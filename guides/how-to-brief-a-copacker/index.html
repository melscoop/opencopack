Outreach

How to brief a co-packer so you can get a quote

Updated 2026-11-19

Plants get a lot of unfinished inquiries. A brief that answers the production questions in one page is the difference between a quote and a shrug. This is a structure you can paste into an email. It is not a contract.

The one-page brief

  1. Who you are — company name, city, and whether this is a first packaged product or an existing brand running out of capacity.
  2. The product — name, flavor count, and a plain-language description (for example, “acidified hot-fill hot sauce in 5-ounce glass”).
  3. The process — hot-fill, cold-fill, kettle cook, retort, bake, dry blend, ferment, refrigerate, freeze. If you already have a scheduled process or process-authority letter, say so and offer to send it.
  4. The pack — format, size, and whether you will supply bottles, pouches, caps, labels, and cartons.
  5. Ingredients — will you source them, or do you need the plant to? Flag allergens.
  6. Volume — the first run you can fund, and a rough 12-month forecast. Use units the plant will recognize (gallons, pounds, or cases).
  7. Certifications required — organic, kosher, gluten-free, SQF customer requirements, and so on. Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves.
  8. Timing — when you need the first delivery, and any hard retail date.
  9. What you want back — a yes/no on fit, a minimum, a ballpark cost per unit, and lead time. You are not asking them to redesign the product in the first email.

Specs that make the brief easier to use

Once a plant is interested, they often want a draft nutrition facts panel, an ingredient list that flags allergens, and enough cost detail to talk about a unit price. Having those ready — even as drafts — keeps the conversation moving. What not to do

  • Do not hide a very small first run. If your first batch is 30 gallons and their published minimum is 100, say so and ask whether a trial is possible.
  • Do not send only a brand story. Plants quote lines, not taglines.
  • Do not assume a shared commercial kitchen and a co-packer are the same thing. A kitchen is space you cook in; a co-packer runs your recipe on their labor and equipment.
