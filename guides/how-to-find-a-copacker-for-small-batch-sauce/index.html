How to find a co-packer for a small-batch sauce

A practical sequence for sauce founders: what “acidified” means for packing, how to read a published minimum, and what to send so a plant can actually quote you.

OpenCopackPublished 2026-11-19Updated 2026-11-19

Sauce founders run into the same wall: a plant will not quote a mystery jar. The work is to make your product easy to say yes or no to, then to call plants that have already said in public that they pack sauces, dressings, or salsas.

1. Know what kind of sauce you have

Many sauces, salsas, and dressings are acidified foods. That usually means a process authority has to review a scheduled process before a plant will run it. Cornell’s Food Venture Center and other university labs do this work; your state extension can point you to a process authority. Do not expect a co-packer to invent that paperwork for a first conversation.

If the sauce is meat-containing, low-acid and shelf-stable, refrigerated, or frozen, say so up front. Those products use different equipment and inspection rules than a hot-fill hot sauce.

2. Filter for plants that already mention sauces

Start with OpenCopack’s sauces and condiments list. Every plant on it has a public source that mentioned sauces, dressings, salsas, marinades, or similar. That is not a guarantee they will take your recipe or your volume.

Prefer plants that have published a minimum, then decide whether you can meet it. “50 gallons” and “1,000 units” are very different asks. If no minimum is published, assume you will have to ask, and that some plants will not answer a short email.

3. Send a brief they can actually quote

Founders on public forums describe waiting months and contacting two dozen plants without a quote. A common reason is a thin brief. Before you write, have:

  • A short description of the product and whether it is acidified, hot-fill, cold-fill, refrigerated, or frozen.
  • Target pack (glass, plastic, pouch, pail) and fill size.
  • The first run size you can actually pay for, and a 12-month volume guess — even if it is modest.
  • Certifications you need (organic, kosher, gluten-free) versus ones that would merely be nice.
  • Whether you will supply ingredients and packaging, or need the plant to source them.
  • Process authority status, if you have it, or a note that you are seeking one.

See also how to brief a co-packer.

4. Expect silence, and keep a log

Capacity is tight at many small plants. If a page says they work with startups or that short runs are welcome, treat that as a reason to try, not as a reservation. Keep a simple log of who you wrote, what you asked, and what they said about minimums and lead time. The plants that do answer are the beginning of a shortlist.

Sources for this guide

  • Public founder threads such as “Stuck in Copacker Hell” on r/smallbusiness, and r/foodscience discussions of how hard co-packer data is to find.
  • Official state directories used in this site (Colorado, Utah, Minnesota), which often publish product types and sometimes a minimum run.
  • Cornell Food Venture Center materials on scheduled processes and acidified foods, and its kitchens/co-packers page at cals.cornell.edu.

This is general information, not legal, food-safety, or business advice. Confirm process, labeling, and inspection rules with a process authority and the plant.
